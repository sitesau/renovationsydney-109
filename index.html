Home Renovations Sydney Checklist Explained

Most renovation stress traces back to decisions made too late. A homeowner who asks for pricing before the scope is settled gets a number that fits an undefined job, and undefined jobs drift. Reading this overview of the mistakes Sydney renovators make shows the same pattern: unclear scope, unchecked approvals and quotes compared on different assumptions.

A checklist reverses that order. It turns the early conversations with a builder into a review of work you have already mapped. The planning material behind this page treats property context, such as a terrace under a conservation overlay, a suburban second-storey addition or a beachside unit kitchen, as different problems rather than interchangeable jobs. Your checklist should reflect that. A home renovations sydney is only useful if it records your property's specifics rather than a generic wish list.

Stage one: map what actually changes

Start with rooms, not moods. The source guidance asks renovators to name the rooms involved, describe what currently feels broken or outdated, and decide whether the layout needs to move. That separation matters because cosmetic work, layout change, structural change and services upgrades price very differently.

  • Write the suburb or postcode, since council pathway and site context shape the next conversation.
  • List each room and whether the work is cosmetic, layout change, structural, services-related or a combination.
  • Note known constraints: access limits, heritage status, apartment or body rules, and any approvals already suspected.
  • Record rough budget expectations so pricing conversations have a frame.

The point is not a finished specification. It is enough shape that a written quote can mean something when it arrives.

Stage two: approvals, structure and compliance

The source material repeatedly places approvals next to structure and finish quality, and that discipline belongs on the checklist. One compliance line is stated plainly: bathroom waterproofing to Australian Standard AS 3740 is non-negotiable for compliant work. Beyond that, the safe reader action is to ask targeted questions instead of assuming a pathway.

  • Ask which approval pathway should be checked before pricing is finalised.
  • Ask how waterproofing, plumbing and electrical changes will be specified in the quote.
  • Ask what documentation will form part of the written contract.

Industry bodies such as the Housing Industry Association and Master Builders Australia publish guidance on engaging builders and understanding contracts, and ASIC Moneysmart covers the budgeting side of renovating. None of these replace advice on your specific property, but they give the checklist credible reference points.

Stage three: cost signals to test a quote

The published cost guidance is deliberately broad, and a checklist should treat it as a sanity check rather than a budget. A cosmetic refresh of a few rooms can sit in the tens of thousands. A whole-home renovation with structural change in Sydney commonly starts around $150,000 and can move into the several hundred thousands.

For kitchens, the guidance separates a budget refresh from a full kitchen with new cabinetry, benchtops, appliances and services updates, with premium custom joinery, integrated appliances and a butler's pantry described as higher again. For bathrooms, the drivers are room size, tiling extent, layout movement and fixture quality; moving plumbing or enlarging the room pushes cost up against a same-layout renovation. Materials are described as the largest single cost, with labour a significant share on top. That makes product selections, cabinetry detail, tile extent and service changes checklist questions, not afterthoughts. The reliable figure remains a written fixed-price quote for the specific property, scope and finish level.

Who this checklist suits

The checklist is most relevant to homeowners still shaping the brief, not readers holding drawings, approvals and a complete specification. It fits owners weighing a whole-home renovation, kitchen upgrade, bathroom renovation, extension, granny flat, or heritage and terrace work, and needing to know what belongs in the first builder conversation.

Apartment owners should make access and body-related constraints visible early. Terrace and heritage-style projects need sharper questions about approvals and existing structure. Owners of project homes or larger suburban houses may be deciding whether a reconfiguration, ground floor extension or first floor addition is the real scope. Across all of these, quote quality depends on the facts supplied before the site visit, which is exactly what the checklist collects.

  1. Write down the property facts. Record suburb or postcode, property type, rooms involved and anything unusual about access, heritage or approvals.
  2. Separate the kinds of work. Mark each item as cosmetic, layout change, structural or services-related, because each prices differently.
  3. Add the compliance line. Confirm bathroom waterproofing will be specified to AS 3740 and ask which approval pathway applies before pricing.
  4. Test quotes against the signals. Compare written fixed-price quotes with the broad cost guides, and check exclusions before any work begins.

Common questions

Is a checklist a substitute for drawings or a specification? No. The source guidance treats the checklist as preparation for the builder conversation, not a replacement for design. It gives the site visit enough shape that a written quote reflects the actual property, scope and finish level.

What is the one compliance item named for bathrooms? Waterproofing to Australian Standard AS 3740. The source material calls it a non-negotiable part of a compliant bathroom, so it belongs on the checklist for any bathroom renovation.

Do the cost figures replace a written quote? No. The tens of thousands and $150,000-plus figures are broad guides. The reliable number is a written fixed-price quote for the specific property, scope and finish level.
